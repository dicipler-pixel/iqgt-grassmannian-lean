11 Discussion and Outlook

The central achievement of this work is the construction of a fully coordinate-free quantum geometric tensor on the Grassmannian manifold of spectral projectors. Starting from the Riesz–Kato theory of isolated spectral subspaces, both the quantum metric and the Berry curvature emerge as natural components of a single intrinsic Hermitian form defined directly on the tangent bundle of \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\). The formulation needs no local eigenvector gauge, no Berry connection and no coordinate chart on parameter space.

A key conceptual point is that the real and imaginary parts of the tensor are not independent objects. Both are generated by the same cross-gap virtual transitions between the spectral subspace of \(\Pi_k\) and its complement. The tangent-space constraint

\[V=\Pi_kV(I-\Pi_k)+(I-\Pi_k)V\Pi_k\]

makes this mechanism explicit and leads directly to the metric–curvature inequality of Section 6. That the metric bounds the curvature is not a coincidence of two quantities but a consequence of their common origin: they are the symmetric and antisymmetric parts of one object built from the same amplitudes. The inequality explains why Berry curvature and the topological phenomena built on it are suppressed when interband mixing is weak or the gap is large, and its control is relative: near a node both sides grow together, so it limits the curvature per unit of metric, not the curvature itself.

The framework also yields a lower bound on non-adiabatic dissipation, and the way it does so is part of the result. The intrinsic metric is the quantum Fisher information of the moving subspace, its information-geometric speed; on its own it measures how much the driving must change, not what that change costs. A bath turns speed into cost. For relaxation to the instantaneous Gibbs state, and equally for a detailed-balance bath, the friction carried by the pairs that move the projector is the intrinsic metric reweighted pair by pair by twice the population difference times the gap times a relaxation factor. At zero temperature this confines the cost to a band of the metric, and the excess work of a protocol is bounded below by the squared intrinsic length divided by the time allowed. The geometric response is thereby connected to thermodynamic irreversibility through an explicit, tested bath weight rather than an assumed proportionality.

From a broader perspective, the construction clarifies the geometric foundations of transport. Higher-order coefficients arise as coordinate-dependent pullbacks of structures intrinsic to the Grassmannian, not as primitive features of the parameter manifold. Section 7 makes this concrete: one sum over cross-gap pairs, read with different powers of the gap, gives the quantum metric, the Berry connection polarizability that drives the intrinsic nonlinear Hall effect, the static response, the rotation friction, the Berry curvature and the orbital-moment kernel. At first order every such quantity factors through the gap. At second order the factorisation acquires a remainder from the rotation of the eigenvectors inside each sector, which is what the exact dipole (7.3) contains, and a direction that does not move the subspace at all can still change how far the other directions move it.

The refraction results of Section 8 deserve a closing comment, since a Snell-type law arising in an operator-theoretic setting invites more interpretation than it can carry. The law is Clairaut's relation for geodesics of the pullback metric, and its content lies in the identification of the index: what refracts a geodesic is a change in the rate at which the occupied subspace rotates. The sine squared that appears is not an additional structure; it is inherited from the fact that the Hilbert–Schmidt distance between projectors is a sum of squared sines of principal angles. The sharpest statements are the two that fix the scope. Where the subspace stops rotating along an interface, the stratum is one projector, it is repulsive, and trajectories turn back by total internal reflection. Where instead the gap narrows while the subspace keeps rotating, the index rises, and trajectories cross and are guided. A fold, where the collapse is transverse and the projector keeps moving along the locus, is crossed freely, and there the degenerate metric does not by itself determine the connection along which transport is computed.

The data of Section 10 close the loop with experiment. In two unrelated systems — the readout of a superconducting heat engine and absorption images of a Bose–Einstein condensate — single leading eigenvectors are free wherever their gap is not resolved: in the engine their large jumps do not repeat across independent halves of the data, and in the condensate a single eigen-image turns by tens of degrees between halves while the subspace containing it moves by what the gap formula predicts, within 0.93–1.12 of it at every resolved gap. That is the reason, measured rather than assumed, for building the geometry on the projector: the vectors trade identity, the subspace keeps it.

Several directions are left open. The non-abelian sector (Remark 4.2) requires lifting the construction from \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\) to the Stiefel bundle, where the traceless part of the curvature lives. Transport through a fold requires a choice of kernel vector field or Ehresmann connection before the geodesic problem can be posed. The mean Uhlmann curvature dipole of Section 9 is written as an operator identity but not evaluated spectrally, and the full nonlinear orbital magnetisation tensor is not derived. On the experimental side, a frame-free version of the readout-tail direction would turn the one remaining Level 0 observation of Section 10 into a Level 1 angle.
