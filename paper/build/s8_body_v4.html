8 Geodesic Refraction Across Degenerate Strata

The metric–curvature inequality governs the magnitude of geometric phase effects but does not prescribe how geometric transport continues across loci where the effective rank of the pulled-back tensor changes. By Proposition 4.1 the pullback metric may degenerate on codimension-one strata of \(M_\Delta\) without the spectral gap closing. The established band-geometry literature assumes this does not happen — the map into the Grassmannian is taken to be an immersion, so that \(\det g\neq0\) throughout. This section asks what governs the geodesics of the pullback metric when that assumption fails. Geodesics are the object studied throughout: they are the paths a transport calculation would follow, not a measurement of transport.

8.1 The refraction law

Suppose \(g\) admits a Killing field tangent to a family of hypersurfaces, the interfaces; let \(x\) be the coordinate along the Killing direction and \(y\) transverse to it, so that \(g=g_\parallel(y)\,dx^2+g_\perp(y)\,dy^2\). Let \(\theta\) be the angle, measured in \(g\), between a trajectory and the interface normal.

Proposition 8.1 (Variational refraction). Along any geodesic of \(g\),

\[\sqrt{g_\parallel}\,\sin\theta=\text{const},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad g_\parallel\sin^2\theta=\text{const}. \tag{8.1}\]

Proof. The Lagrangian \(g_\parallel\dot x^2+g_\perp\dot y^2\) is independent of \(x\), so \(p_x=g_\parallel\dot x\) is conserved. For a unit-speed geodesic the along-interface component of the velocity is \(\sin\theta=\sqrt{g_\parallel}\,\dot x\), whence \(p_x=\sqrt{g_\parallel}\sin\theta\). Squaring gives the second form. ∎

Across two sides of an interface, (8.1) is the sine-squared refraction law \(R_1\sin^2\theta_1=R_2\sin^2\theta_2\), with rigidity \(R=g_\parallel\). It is Clairaut's relation, and therefore Snell's law with refractive index \(n=\sqrt{g_\parallel}\). Three consequences fix the reading. The sine-squared form is the square of ordinary Snell, not an independent law. There is no separate index to introduce: Fermat's principle with \(g\) as the metric yields (8.1) directly. And the quantity playing the role of the index is \(g_\parallel\), the metric component along the interface — not the smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\min}\) of \(g\), and not its reciprocal. Near a degenerate stratum the collapsing direction is the along-interface direction, so \(\lambda_{\min}\) and \(g_\parallel\) coincide there; a rule written with \(\lambda_{\min}\) is correct in the regime where it is usually applied and fails away from it (Figure {{REF:candidates}}).

8.2 The index is a subspace rotation rate

Proposition 8.2. For subspaces \(\mathrm{Ran}(\Pi_1),\mathrm{Ran}(\Pi_2)\) with principal angles \(\vartheta_i\), \(\|\Pi_1-\Pi_2\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=2\sum_i\sin^2\vartheta_i\). Since \(\|\delta\Pi\|^2_{\mathrm{HS}}=2g_\Pi(\delta\Pi,\delta\Pi)\), \(\sqrt g=\sin\vartheta\) to leading order for a single principal angle, and the refractive index \(n=\sqrt{g_\parallel}\) is the rate at which the occupied subspace rotates as one moves along the interface.

Refraction in this setting is therefore not an optical analogy imported from outside. The trajectory bends because the subspace rotates at different rates on the two sides of the stratum, and \(\sin^2\) appears because the Hilbert–Schmidt metric on \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\) is itself a sum of squared sines of principal angles.

8.3 Total internal reflection

Proposition 8.3 (Total internal reflection at a degenerate stratum). Let \(C=g_\parallel\sin^2\theta\) be the conserved quantity of (8.1). Since \(\sin^2\theta\le1\), a geodesic can only visit points where \(g_\parallel\ge C\). A trajectory with \(C>0\) cannot reach a stratum on which \(g_\parallel\to0\): it turns at the locus \(g_\parallel=C\), where \(\sin\theta=1\) and the velocity is tangent to the interface. Only trajectories with \(C=0\), already exactly normal, reach the stratum.

The degenerate stratum is repulsive: generic transport does not approach it, and is turned away before reaching it. This is the directional protection of the geometry, correctly stated; the mechanism is the caustic of a metric whose along-interface component vanishes, the same as total internal reflection in optics.

8.4 A solvable test

Take the two-band operator \(\Sigma=\hat{\mathbf d}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) with \(\hat{\mathbf d}(x,y)=(\sin y\cos kx,\ \sin y\sin kx,\ \cos y)\), \(k=3\), so that the spectral gap equals 2 everywhere: any degeneration of the pulled-back metric is failure of immersion, not a closing gap. The metric computed from \(\Pi\) by central differences is \(g=\mathrm{diag}\big(\tfrac14k^2\sin^2y,\ \tfrac14\big)\) to \(5\times10^{-10}\), and \(\sqrt{g_\parallel}\,dx\) equals the principal angle between \(\Pi(x,y)\) and \(\Pi(x+dx,y)\), computed from eigenvector overlaps with no trace identity, to \(10^{-8}\) (Proposition 8.2). The full geodesic system, integrated from \(y=0.15\) at \(\theta=60^\circ\) with no conservation law imposed, crosses the branch switch of \(\lambda_{\min}\) at \(\sin y=1/k\); along it \(g_\parallel\sin^2\theta\) drifts by \(1.7\times10^{-12}\), while \(\lambda_{\min}\sin^2\theta\) drifts by \(1.3\) and \(\sin^2\theta/\lambda_{\min}\) by \(3.3\). A geodesic launched inward from \(y=0.60\) at \(\theta=35^\circ\) has \(C=0.23600011\) and a predicted turning point \(y=0.32981248\); the integrated trajectory turns at \(y=0.32981248\) with \(\sin\theta=1.00000000\) — grazing incidence, not normal. Over incidences from \(20^\circ\) to \(89^\circ\) every trajectory turns before the stratum; the exactly normal one passes through it.

Remark 8.4 (What bends a geodesic is a metric dipole). The geodesic equation \(\ddot a^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot a^i\dot a^j=0\) is driven by the Christoffel symbols, and those of the first kind are half-sums of derivatives of the metric: \[\Gamma_{ij,k}=\tfrac12\big(G_{ik,j}+G_{jk,i}-G_{ij,k}\big),\qquad G_{ij,k}=\partial_kg_{ij}. \tag{8.2}\] \(G_{ij,k}\) is the quantum metric dipole of Section 7.1. In the coordinates of Proposition 8.1 the only transverse force is \(\Gamma_{xx,y}=-\tfrac12\partial_yg_\parallel\), and in the test above, computed from the projector by finite differences, it agrees with the dipole to \(3\times10^{-8}\). The bending of this section and the dipole responses of Section 7 are therefore the same derivative of the same metric, read once as a force on a path and once as a transport coefficient.

8.5 Two kinds of degenerate stratum

Let \(L\) be a connected interface on which \(g\) is degenerate, with \(x\) along \(L\) and \(y\) across it. Which direction collapses decides everything that follows.

Lemma 8.5 (A stratum on which nothing moves). On \(L\), \(g_\parallel\equiv0\) if and only if \(\partial_x\Pi\equiv0\), if and only if \(\Pi\) is constant along \(L\): the whole interface maps to one point of \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\).

Proof. \(g_\parallel=g_\Pi(\partial_x\Pi,\partial_x\Pi)=\tfrac12\|\partial_x\Pi\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2\) vanishes only when \(\partial_x\Pi=0\). A curve with zero derivative on a connected interval is constant. The converse is immediate. ∎

So a degenerate stratum is one of two kinds. It is polar if the kernel of \(g\) is tangent to it: then, by Lemma 8.5, \(\Pi\) is constant along it, and the refraction law with its total internal reflection applies. The stratum of Section 8.4 is polar: the whole line \(y=0\) is one projector. It is a fold if the kernel points across it while \(\Pi\) keeps moving along it, as on the Bloch sphere with \(\theta=y+1.1,\ \phi=x^2/2\), where \(\partial_x\Pi=0\) on \(x=0\) while \(|\partial_y\Pi|=0.49\). The two are the null-hypersurface and caustic cases of the singularity theory of maps.

The first-order metric cannot tell the two apart beyond the direction of its kernel. The next order can.

Proposition 8.6 (Second order along a silent direction). Let \(v\) be a silent direction at a point, \(\partial_v\Pi=0\). Then \(\partial_v^2\Pi\) is block off-diagonal, so it is itself a tangent vector, and along the line through the point in direction \(v\)

\[d(\varepsilon)=\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\sqrt{g_\Pi\big(\partial_v^2\Pi,\partial_v^2\Pi\big)}+O(\varepsilon^3), \tag{8.3}\]

where \(d=\|\Pi(\varepsilon)-\Pi\|_{\mathrm{HS}}/\sqrt2\) is the chordal distance of Proposition 8.2.

Proof. Differentiating \(\Pi^2=\Pi\) twice gives \(\Pi''=\Pi''\Pi+2\Pi'\Pi'+\Pi\Pi''\). With \(\Pi'=0\) this is \(\Pi''=\Pi''\Pi+\Pi\Pi''\), the tangent law of Section 2; multiplying by \(\Pi\) on both sides gives \(\Pi\Pi''\Pi=2\Pi\Pi''\Pi\), so \(\Pi\Pi''\Pi=0\), and multiplying by \(I-\Pi\) on both sides gives \((I-\Pi)\Pi''(I-\Pi)=0\). Taylor's theorem gives \(\Pi(\varepsilon)-\Pi=\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}2\Pi''+O(\varepsilon^3)\), and \(\|\Pi''\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2=2g_\Pi(\Pi'',\Pi'')\). ∎

This sorts the strata by order. Along a polar stratum \(\Pi\) is silent at every order. Across a fold it is silent at first order and moves at second: the distance grows as \(\varepsilon^2\), not \(\varepsilon\). In the fold above, at \(x=0\), \(\partial_x\Pi=0\) exactly, the diagonal blocks of \(\partial_x^2\Pi\) vanish to \(5\times10^{-17}\), and the ratio of \(d(\varepsilon)\) to the leading term of (8.3) is \(0.999999\), \(1.000000\), \(1.000000\) at \(\varepsilon=0.1,0.03,0.01\); along the polar stratum the projector drifts by \(10^{-15}\) over the whole line (Figure {{REF:orders}}). The metric is a quadratic form with a kernel, and what happens in the kernel is decided at the next order: a fold opens at fourth order in the squared distance, a polar stratum never opens.

Remark 8.7 (What a fold needs). The Christoffel symbols (8.2) are contracted with \(g^{-1}\), and both kinds of stratum make them diverge: at the polar stratum of Section 8.4, \(\Gamma^x_{xy}=\cot y\); at the fold above, where \(g=\mathrm{diag}\big(\tfrac14\sin^2(y+1.1)\,x^2,\tfrac14\big)\), \(\Gamma^x_{xx}=1/x\). At a polar stratum this is harmless, because the conserved quantity of (8.1) keeps every trajectory with \(C>0\) away from it. At a fold nothing does: the Clairaut constant contains \(g_\parallel\) only, \(g_\parallel\) does not vanish there, trajectories reach the fold at every incidence, and the geodesics of \(g\) alone are not determined across it. The geodesic problem must be supplemented by a choice of direction in the kernel, which the degenerate metric does not supply. The same structure is basic in Carrollian geometry, where a degenerate metric describes a null hypersurface only together with a vector field in its kernel [Ciambelli–Jai-akson 2025]. Only the polar case is governed by Proposition 8.3.

Example 8.8 (Three bodies seen through their distances). Place three equal masses in the plane at \(q_1,q_2,q_3\in\mathbb C\), and form the Jacobi vectors \(\rho_1=(q_2-q_1)/\sqrt2\), \(\rho_2=(2q_3-q_1-q_2)/\sqrt6\) and the vector \(\psi=(\rho_1,\rho_2)\in\mathbb C^2\). The projector \(\Pi=\psi\psi^*/|\psi|^2\in\mathrm{Gr}(1,\mathbb C^2)\) forgets the overall size and the planar rotation, which is the phase of \(\psi\), and keeps exactly the shape of the triangle: \(\mathrm{Gr}(1,\mathbb C^2)\) is the shape sphere of the three-body problem [Montgomery 2015]. Its Bloch vector \(w=(|\rho_1|^2-|\rho_2|^2,\ 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\rho_1\bar\rho_2,\ 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\rho_1\bar\rho_2)/|\psi|^2\) has third component proportional to the signed area, so the collinear triangles form the great circle \(w_3=0\).

Now record the shape only through the normalised squared distances \(r_{ij}^2/\sum r^2\). They are linear in \(|\rho_1|^2\), \(|\rho_2|^2\) and \(\mathrm{Re}\,\rho_1\bar\rho_2\), so they see \(w_1\) and \(w_2\) and not the sign of \(w_3\): a triangle and its mirror image, \(w_3\mapsto-w_3\), have the same distances (difference exactly 0). The metric that the distances pull back to the sphere is degenerate exactly on the collinear circle, where the ratio of its singular values is 0 (it is 0.24 at latitude 0.4). Its kernel points straight across the circle (latitude component \(1.000000\)), while \(\Pi\) keeps moving along it (\(|\partial\Pi|\ge0.707\)). To an observer who sees only distances, the collinear triangles are a fold: the two mirror sheets of the sphere meet there, and the direction that observer cannot see is the orientation of the triangle. The three-body motion itself runs on the nondegenerate round metric of the shape sphere, inherited from the mass metric, and that metric supplies exactly the structure the fold lacks (Figure {{REF:shape}}).

8.6 When the gap narrows instead

The refraction law asks only for the pullback metric, so it applies unchanged when the index varies because the gap varies rather than because the image of the family folds. What changes is the direction of the effect. By Lemma 3.1 a fixed coupling divided by a smaller gap moves the projector faster, so where the gap narrows \(g_\parallel\) rises and the index rises with it. Trajectories then bend toward the normal, cross the narrow-gap line, and are turned back only where the index has fallen to their conserved level on either side: the narrow-gap line guides them as a graded-index fibre guides light.

In the family \(\Sigma(x,y)=\cos(3x)\,\sigma_x+\sin(3x)\,\sigma_y+2.5y\,\sigma_z\), the gap \(2\sqrt{1+6.25y^2}\) is narrowest on \(y=0\) and the occupied projector never stops rotating: \(g_\parallel=9/\big(4(1+6.25y^2)\big)\). A geodesic launched from \(y=0.8\) at \(50^\circ\) conserves \(g_\parallel\sin^2\theta\) to \(1.1\times10^{-11}\) (the \(\lambda_{\min}\) candidate drifts by \(4.4\)), crosses \(y=0\), and turns at \(y=-1.0969\), where the conserved level predicts \(1.0970\) (Figure {{REF:narrow}}). Total internal reflection is therefore a property of strata on which the subspace stops rotating, not of places where the gap closes.
