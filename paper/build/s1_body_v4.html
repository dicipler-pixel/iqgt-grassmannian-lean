Abstract

We construct a fully coordinate-free formulation of the Quantum Geometric Tensor on the Grassmannian manifold \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\) of rank-\(k\) orthogonal projectors associated with an isolated spectral subspace of a self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space \(\mathcal H\). Starting from Kato's analytic perturbation theory and the Riesz resolvent formula, we define an intrinsic Hermitian form \(Q_\Pi(V,W)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi VW)\) on the tangent bundle of the Grassmannian. Its symmetric real part is the intrinsic Hilbert–Schmidt quantum metric and its skew-symmetric imaginary part is the Berry curvature 2-form. Every tangent deformation is generated by transitions across the spectral gap, and both components admit exact spectral decompositions in terms of inter-band matrix elements weighted by inverse gaps. We prove the sharp metric–curvature inequality in operator form: the determinant inequality \(4\det g\ge\Omega^2\) of band geometry, extended to any pair of tangent directions on the Grassmannian, with its extremal partner given explicitly. For dissipation, the metric is the quantum Fisher information of the moving subspace, and under an explicit bath model the friction on projector rotations is the metric reweighted by the spectrum, which bounds the excess work of finite-time driving from below by the squared intrinsic length over the time allowed; the friction weight is checked against slow-driving simulations of two different baths. Transport tensors, including the quantum metric dipole, the nonlinear Hall response and the orbital moment, arise as pullbacks of one cross-gap pair sum read with different powers of the gap. Across degenerate strata, geodesics of the pullback metric obey a refraction law whose index is the rate at which the subspace rotates: strata where the subspace stops rotating reflect trajectories, while lines where the gap narrows guide them. The construction extends to mixed states through the symmetric logarithmic derivative. Applied to public data from a superconducting heat engine and a Bose–Einstein condensate, a three-level standard for extracting angles shows that single eigenvectors trade identity where their gap is unresolved while the subspace keeps it. These data test the stability of the subspace, the reason for building the geometry on the projector, rather than the tensor itself. The Davis–Kahan bound, read as a rule for what can be looked at separately and what only as a whole, ties every result together.

1 Introduction and Foundational Underpinnings

The variation of eigenstates under deformations of a self-adjoint operator is a central theme in mathematical physics. The rigorous framework for tracking such variations was established by Tosio Kato through his development of analytic perturbation theory for linear operators, building on the functional calculus and resolvent methods of Frigyes Riesz [Kato 1995]. Kato showed that when a finite subset of the spectrum remains isolated from the rest by a contour in the complex plane, the corresponding spectral projector varies analytically with the operator. This projector-valued formulation provides a globally smooth description of spectral subspaces and naturally circumvents the technical difficulties associated with individual, possibly degenerate, eigenvectors.

In modern condensed matter and quantum information physics, the geometric consequences of this spectral sensitivity are captured by the Quantum Geometric Tensor [Provost–Vallee 1980; Berry 1984]. Conventionally, the tensor is introduced parametrically on a manifold of external parameters, such as crystal momentum in the Brillouin zone. Its imaginary part yields the Berry curvature, whose integral over closed manifolds produces topological invariants, while its real part defines a Riemannian quantum metric that governs fidelity susceptibility, orbital magnetism and Wannier localisation.

Despite its success, the parametric formulation inherently mixes two distinct geometric structures: the intrinsic geometry of the state manifold and the arbitrary choice of coordinates on the parameter space. To disentangle them, we develop a coordinate-free formulation in which the full tensor is defined intrinsically on the tangent bundle of the Grassmannian manifold \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,\mathcal H)\) of rank-\(k\) orthogonal projectors. Using the Riesz–Kato resolvent identity, we show that both the quantum metric and the Berry curvature emerge directly from a single canonical Hermitian form on this manifold.

A central result of this approach is that every admissible tangent deformation of a spectral projector is generated exclusively by virtual transitions across the spectral gap. This structural constraint leads to exact spectral decompositions of both the metric and the curvature in terms of interband matrix elements weighted by inverse energy gaps. It also implies a sharp inequality relating the Berry curvature to the quantum metric, establishing that geometric phase effects are quantitatively bounded by the strength of cross-gap susceptibility.

Furthermore, we show that the intrinsic quantum metric controls the cost of non-adiabatic driving. The metric is the quantum Fisher information of the moving subspace; a bath converts that speed into dissipation, and the friction carried by the pairs that rotate the projector is the intrinsic metric reweighted pair by pair by the spectrum and the bath. The excess work generated by finite-rate driving is then bounded from below by the thermodynamic length measured with this metric, with an explicit spectral prefactor. This provides a direct geometric origin for dissipative costs in driven quantum systems.

By anchoring the construction in operator theory rather than band parameters, the framework reveals that physical parameter spaces do not generate quantum geometry; they select finite-dimensional trajectories through a pre-existing Grassmannian geometry. Two consequences follow that are invisible in coordinates. Transport tensors of every order are pullbacks of one cross-gap structure, so that the quantum metric dipole, the nonlinear Hall response and the orbital moment are the same pair sum weighted differently. And where the pullback degenerates, the intrinsic geometry decides what happens: geodesics of the pullback metric refract with an index equal to the rate at which the subspace rotates, is reflected where that rotation stops, and is guided where the gap narrows instead.

Several statements here are coordinate-free recastings of established results, and we say so where they appear: the metric–curvature inequality is the determinant inequality of band geometry and its saturation the Kähler or ideal-band condition; the pullback description of the band metric and curvature is standard; the stability of subspaces against perturbation is the Davis–Kahan theorem; the redistribution flow is Brockett's double-bracket flow. Each is included because the projector formulation makes its proof, or its scope, transparent, and because later sections rest on it.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the intrinsic quantum geometric tensor on the Grassmannian. Section 3 derives its explicit spectral realisation. Section 4 shows how conventional parametric expressions arise as pullbacks and where the pullback degenerates. Section 5 establishes the spectral lower bound on dissipation. Section 6 derives the metric–curvature inequality and its geometric consequences. Section 7 builds the transport tensors, Section 8 treats geodesic refraction across degenerate strata, and Section 9 extends the construction to mixed states.
